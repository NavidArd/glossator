3   Lie Groups and the Language of Symmetry

3.4 Generators and the Lie Algebra

A symmetry is a transformation that leaves something unchanged. When the transformations can be varied continuously, as with rotations by an arbitrary angle, we can look at those that differ only infinitesimally from doing nothing at all. Every group element close to the identity can then be written as

$$ g(\epsilon) = I + \epsilon\, X + \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2), $$

where $X$ is called a generator of the group. Repeating the infinitesimal step $N$ times and letting $N\to\infty$ with $\theta = N\epsilon$ fixed, we recover a finite transformation,

$$ g(\theta) = \lim_{N\to\infty}\Big(I + \frac{\theta}{N} X\Big)^{N} = e^{\theta X}. $$

For rotations in the plane, $R(\theta)$ is generated by a single matrix. Differentiating at $\theta=0$ gives

$$ R(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{pmatrix}, \qquad X = \frac{dR}{d\theta}\Big|_{\theta=0} = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -1\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}. $$

The generators of a group are not independent objects floating around: their commutators close back on the set. For the rotation group in three dimensions one finds

$$ [J_i, J_j] = i\,\epsilon_{ijk}\, J_k , $$

and these relations define the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{su}(2)$. Remarkably, almost everything about the group near the identity is encoded in these structure constants $\epsilon_{ijk}$.

Noether's theorem, informally

Suppose the action $S=\int dt\, L(q,\dot q)$ is invariant under $q \to q + \epsilon\,\delta q$. Then the quantity

$$ Q = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q}\,\delta q $$

is conserved along every solution of the equations of motion, $\frac{dQ}{dt}=0$. Invariance under time translations gives energy, under spatial translations momentum, and under rotations angular momentum. In field theory the same reasoning yields a conserved current with $\partial_\mu J^\mu = 0$, so that the charge $Q=\int d^3x\, J^0$ does not change in time.

3.5   Representations

A representation assigns to every group element a linear map, $g \mapsto D(g)$, such that $D(g_1)D(g_2)=D(g_1 g_2)$. The same abstract group can act on vectors of very different sizes. The spin-$\tfrac12$ representation of $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ uses the Pauli matrices,

$$ \sigma_1=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad \sigma_2=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\ i&0\end{pmatrix},\quad \sigma_3=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}, $$

with $J_i = \tfrac12 \sigma_i$. A rotation by $2\pi$ then gives $e^{2\pi i J_3} = -I$: spinors change sign, and only a rotation by $4\pi$ brings them back to themselves.

The Casimir operator $J^2 = J_1^2+J_2^2+J_3^2$ commutes with every generator, and its eigenvalue $j(j+1)$ labels the irreducible representations, of dimension $2j+1$.
